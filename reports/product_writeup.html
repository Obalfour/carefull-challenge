Product writeup

Carefull transaction alert pipeline · how I approached the problem, what I traded off, and what I would build next
The idea in one paragraph
The data showed that "suspicious" depends on who the person is: a $2,000 Zelle payment is routine for one user and an emergency for another. So the system has two halves. Cheap, transparent rules look for the shape of each fraud pattern and are tuned to miss nothing. An LLM reads each flagged transaction next to the user's profile and history and decides whether it is genuinely concerning, explains why in words a caregiver can understand, and says how sure it is.

1. What the data told me (notebooks)

  • Three real incidents, two decoys. Margaret sends growing amounts to a new payee, Dorothy forwards ~94–95% of large deposits from unknown companies within ~30 h, and Helen's card suddenly shows nightlife and luxury hotels in Spain, Thailand and the UAE. Robert (golf, London trips, $2,000 a month to his wife) and Frank (gifts to grandchildren) look unusual but are labeled benign.
  • Size relative to the person separates well. Benign debits have a median of ~2.5% of monthly income (max ~40%); alerts a median of ~27% (up to ~270%).
  • Generic anomaly detection is noisy. A 7-day rolling baseline highlighted 78 days, only 17 with a real alert, and still flagged 20 days for Robert and 16 for Frank, who have none.
  • Recipients need cleaning before they can be counted. The same person appears as KEVIN R, KEVIN RICH and KEVIN RICHARDS; 37 transfers held 13 spellings of 8 people. Without that, "new payee with growing amounts" is invisible.

  • Fix false positives at the source first. My first weekly rule flagged every debit in an expensive week, including the usual grocery store: 146 flags, 122 benign. Limiting it to first-time merchants removed most of them. check_rules.py makes this loop fast: change a threshold in .env, see true and false positives per rule, no API calls.
  • Give the LLM the person, not just the transaction. The prompt carries the full profile, the rule evidence with numbers, the user's established behavior and the pattern-specific history. The "established behavior" window stops 30 days before the transaction, so an ongoing incident (Helen's trip) cannot become the baseline that excuses itself.
  • Make it argue both sides. The output schema asks for evidence for and against risk before the verdict. That is what lets it say "the $2,000 to Patricia Chen is to his wife, who manages household expenses" instead of reacting to the amount.
  • Never drop uncertainty silently. Confidence below 0.6 becomes "suspicious + human review"; if the LLM fails, the rule label is kept and reviewed. A missed alert costs more than a review.

4. Tradeoffs I made

DecisionWhat I gainedWhat I gave up
Rules tuned for recall, LLM for precisionEvery case reaches the LLM; rules stay simple and auditable. The LLM can only judge what the rules flag: rule recall is the ceiling.
One LLM call per flagged transactionSimple, per-transaction labels to evaluate and explain. Helen's 12 purchases cost 12 calls for one story; pattern-level calls would be cheaper.
gemini-3.1-flash-lite~$11/month at 50k transactions, 2.6 s median latency, no errors here. Not compared yet against a larger model (free-tier quota); untested on harder, ambiguous cases.
Calendar-week totals for the weekly ruleMatches how I explored the data; easy to explain. Can fire up to 7 days late. Production needs a rolling 7-day window.
Thresholds chosen on the labeled dataFast iteration with 5 users and 32 positives. No held-out set: the perfect score is optimistic, not a forecast.

5. Product considerations

  • Escalate gradually, respect autonomy. The LLM rated Margaret's first $200 payment to Kevin "medium: monitor" and moved to "high: alert" as the amounts grew. In the product, "medium" should mean a gentle check-in with the user, and only "high" should reach the adult children. Alerting on the first payment would feel patronizing; waiting until $2,000 would be too late.
  • The explanation is the product. Each alert carries a one- or two-sentence summary written for a caregiver ("deposit from an unknown business, almost immediately forwarded to an unfamiliar person") plus the evidence. A caregiver needs to know what to ask, not a risk score.
  • Group alerts by incident. Dorothy has 12 alerting transactions but one story. Caregivers should get one message per incident, updated as it develops.
  • Profiles are a feature. The biography is what clears Robert and Frank. Onboarding should collect who the user regularly pays (family, caregivers, contractors) and planned travel.

6. What I would build next

Evaluation

  • A held-out set: synthetic users and scenarios generated from the same patterns, plus hard negatives (a user who really does start paying a new contractor more each week).
  • Run the comparison with gemini-3.8-flash and a non-Google model on those harder cases, where models would actually differ.
  • Check whether LLM confidence is calibrated, and tune the review threshold on data with real ambiguity.

System

  • Streaming with rolling windows, and pattern-level validation (one call per incident, updated as it grows).
  • More patterns: new payee + wire + urgency, gift cards and crypto ATMs, sudden changes in login or contact data.
  • Orchestrate the steps with LangGraph, add Batch API and prompt caching, and log prompts and verdicts so caregiver feedback becomes labeled data.
